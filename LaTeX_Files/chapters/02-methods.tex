One of the methods of preprocessing in MRI images is the bias field correction. The bias fields is a 
low-frequency spatially varying MRI artifact resulting from spatial inhomogeneity of the magnetic field, variations in the sensitivity of the reception coil, and the interaction between the magnetic field and the human body.

\subsection{Foundation model DINOv2}
\label{DINO}
DINOv2 is an advanced self-superviced learning technique, released 2023 by MetaAI. This foundation model is particular powerful in the fields of computer vision. 
It has a high accuracy in identifying including objects within iamges and video frames.
The model extracts the features directly from the images, and does not rely on text descriptions.
The architecture of the model is decided into 2 levels. 
The first level is the Image-level objective. 
The initial DINO architecture, consists of a student-teacher networks, that includes a larger network, the teacher, and smaller network, the student. 
Both of the network crop parts of the image, and feed that to the feature encoder. The feature encoder, will produce
embeddings, which will be further used. The student network is build by training on the embeddings. 
And the Teacher network is build by taking advantage of the weights of different student models.
The second level design is the path-level design. The vision transformer devices the image into 4x4 batches, unrolls the array and
randomly masks some of the input patches, that student network trains on. Principal component analysis (PCAs) are computed for patch features extracted by the network.

\section{Comparison of Different Levels of Analysis}
\label{sec:statistics}

In this project, different methodological approaches were investigated to evaluate the influence of data representation and aggregation on classification performance. The experiments were designed to compare slice-level and patient-level classification strategies, as well as the integration of information from different anatomical orientations.

Initially, individual MRI slices were analysed independently using their corresponding feature representations. Classification was performed separately for each anatomical orientation (axial, coronal, and sagittal). Additionally, a combined-orientation approach was investigated, in which the slice-level feature representations from all three orientations were concatenated into a single dataset before classification.

To investigate the effect of patient-level feature aggregation, a second approach was implemented. In this configuration, all feature vectors belonging to the same patient and anatomical orientation were averaged to obtain a single patient-level representation. These aggregated feature vectors were subsequently used as input to the machine learning classifiers.

Furthermore, prediction-level aggregation was considered as an alternative strategy, in which the class probabilities obtained from individual slices are averaged across all slices belonging to the same patient. This allows patient-level predictions to be derived from independently classified slices.

The overall objective of these experiments was to determine whether aggregating information across multiple MRI slices improves classification performance compared with independent slice-level analysis. In particular, the comparison aimed to evaluate the potential benefits of patient-level feature aggregation and the integration of information from different anatomical orientations.
